The Case Against a Biblical Hero: The Problem of Samson

Samson, often exalted as a heroic figure and judge of Israel, exemplifies the inconsistency and alteration embedded within biblical narratives.

A careful reading—especially from the Septuagint, the the earliest known Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures—reveals an individual whose behavior was described in the text as engaging in actions that conflict with Nazirite vows and certain legal expectations[1], and clearly at odds with the laws he was supposedly ordained to uphold. Modern translations often present a sanitized portrayal of Samson's offenses, softening and masking the reality of who he was.

The Story of Elisha and the Children

Elisha has just witnessed the miraculous ascent of his mentor Elijah into the heavens.

Now the sole prophetic figure, Elisha is walking toward Bethel—a city already known for its idolatry.

The Tower of Babel -

A Hebrew Reimagining of Babylonian Ziggurats

The Historical Reality:

Ziggurats were massive stepped temple towers built throughout Mesopotamia starting around 2100 BCE.

These were literal "towers reaching to heaven" - multi-story religious complexes designed to connect earth and the divine realm. The most famous was the Etemenanki ziggurat in Babylon, dedicated to the god Marduk.

The Biblical Reframe:

The Hebrew writers took this well-known Babylonian architectural tradition and turned it into a cautionary tale about human pride. Instead of celebrating these monuments as sacred connections to the gods, they recast them as symbols of arrogance - humans trying to "make a name for themselves" and "reach heaven" through their own efforts.

The Political Context:

This story was suggested by some scholars to have been written during or after the Babylonian exile (586 BCE)

Hebrew writers were processing their captivity in Babylon. They're literally surrounded by these massive ziggurats, and some scholars interpret this as a narrative intended to explain why Babylon has all these tower-temples while simultaneously delegitimizing them as monuments to human arrogance rather than divine connection.

Textual Manipulation

What happened next was textual modification. Jewish scribes, working centuries after Christ, created the Masoretic Text—a heavily edited, standardized version that intentionally diverged according to certain scholarly interpretations from the Septuagint to distance Judaism from Christian interpretations. Variations were eliminated, prophecies were altered, and entire passages were modified.

This altered Masoretic version became the foundation for virtually every Bible sitting in churches today. The King James Bible (1611) primarily focuses on these later, adulterated Hebrew texts rather than the authentic Septuagint tradition used by the apostles themselves.

Modern translations—the NIV, ESV, NASB, and the New World Translation—all trace their Old Testament lineage back to these same later standardized manuscripts, not to the Bible that Jesus and the early church actually used.

Disproving the Flat Earth

Ship Disappearance Over Horizon

As ships sail away, they disappear bottom-first, not all at once. This only makes sense if there's curvature — on a flat plane, they'd just get smaller and maintain their proportions. You can test this at the ocean with binoculars or a camera.

Eratosthenes' Shadow Experiment (240 BCE)

In Egypt, Eratosthenes measured the sun's angle in two cities 500 miles apart on the same day. One city (Syene) had no shadow at noon, while the other (Alexandria) had a 7.2° shadow. He used simple geometry to calculate Earth's circumference and got it nearly spot-on — 25,000 miles. You can recreate this using sticks and shadows.

Airplane Flight Paths

The shortest route between two distant cities is a curved path on a flat map — because maps are flattened versions of a globe. These flight paths match great circles on a globe, not straight lines on flat maps.

Curvature Drop Formula

You can calculate how much the Earth "drops" over a distance: Drop (in feet) approximately equals 8 times (miles) squared. Example: at 10 miles away, an object would be 800 feet below the horizon (assuming no elevation). This explains why tall buildings or mountains vanish bottom-up from far distances.

Time Zones and Star Movements

If the Earth were flat, the sun would rise and set at the same time everywhere — it doesn't.

Time zones work because of a rotating sphere. Stars also rotate around the celestial poles, and different constellations appear in the northern vs. southern hemisphere.

Earths Rotation

A ring laser gyroscope (RLG) detects rotational movement — specifically, angular velocity — by using the interference of two laser beams traveling in opposite directions around a closed loop. If the device is rotating, one beam will have to travel slightly farther than the other due to the Sagnac effect. The interference pattern (called a "beat frequency") reveals the rate and axis of rotation.

How Does This Relate to Earth's Rotation?

If Earth is spinning, the laser ring gyro — when fixed to the surface — will detect a constant angular rotation of approximately 15 degrees per hour, corresponding to Earth's 360°/24h spin. That's exactly what is observed in highly sensitive RLGs.

Proof That the Earth, Not the Sun, Is Moving

If the Earth were stationary and the Sun was orbiting us, a laser gyro on Earth should not detect any rotation — because the Earth wouldn't be spinning. But it does. Always. The angular rotation detected by the laser ring gyro is not coming from the stars or the Sun — it's consistent with the Earth rotating on its own axis.

Real-World Example: Bob Knodel's Gyro

This was featured in the 2018 documentary Behind the Curve, where a flat-earther used a $20,000 fiber optic gyroscope. His team expected zero rotation if the Earth was flat and still. Result? The gyro detected 15°/hour rotation — exactly matching Earth's spin.

He said: "We were taken aback. We didn't expect that." They tried to "shield" it in various ways (placing it in a container called a "Faraday cage") to blame the stars or celestial energy — but nothing changed the result.

The Name “Jehovah”

The name "Jehovah" is a late invention based on a misinterpretation and mispronunciation of the original Hebrew name YHWH. This name was considered too sacred to pronounce, so Jews began saying "Adonai," meaning "Lord," whenever they saw YHWH written. Around the 7th–10th centuries CE, Jewish scribes (Masoretes) added vowel points to the consonantal Hebrew text to preserve pronunciation.

But for YHWH, they inserted the vowels of "Adonai"—a replacement word—under the letters YHWH, not to indicate how to pronounce the name, but as a reminder to say Adonai instead. So you'd get something like this hybrid form:

YeHoWaH

Medieval Christian scholars didn't understand that this was a mash-up and mistakenly combined the consonants of YHWH with the vowels of Adonai, resulting in: YeHoWaH. In Latinized form: Jehovah (since Latin had no "Y," it used "J," and "W" became "V").

Biblical scholar Marvin Pope described the spelling "Jehovah" as a "morphological monstrosity with no claim to legitimacy except the several centuries of misguided usage". This quote is often interpreted as referring to "Jehovah" as a "linguistic frankenstein"

—a false hybrid of consonants from YHWH, vowels from Adonai, creating the invented name: Jehovah.

Burial was a privilege, not given to executed criminals or rebels.

Bible Claim:

Jesus is buried the same day in a private tomb by a Sanhedrin member, Joseph of Arimathea (Mark 15:43–46).

Contradiction:

This violates Roman custom — rebels (especially those crucified) didn't get honored burials.

Also contradicts Jewish burial customs which would not allow touching a corpse before Sabbath.

Treatment by Romans

Historical Fact:

Roman soldiers were severe and did not care about Jewish religious customs.

Once sentenced, a person was dehumanized, publicly humiliated, and discarded.

Bible Claim:

Romans respect Jewish customs (breaking legs before Sabbath, giving body to a Jew for burial).

Pilate is portrayed as hesitant, even sympathetic (John 19:12–16).

Contradiction:

Romans didn't give a damn about appeasing Jewish tradition, and Pilate was known historically for cruelty, not hesitation.

The Crown of Thorns and Mockery

Historical Fact:

While mockery happened, the crown of thorns is not recorded in any Roman practice.

Dressing up a condemned man as a "king" was highly unusual unless it was state propaganda.

Bible Claim:

Soldiers create a crown of thorns, purple robe, and mock Jesus as "King of the Jews" (Matthew 27:29).

Contradiction:

This feels theatrical and symbolic, likely added for theological meaning, not historical accuracy.

The Presence of Women and Followers at the Cross

Historical Fact:

Crucifixions were held in isolated, guarded places.

No family or friends were allowed near the cross — it was dangerous, humiliating, and tightly controlled.

Bible Claim:

Jesus' mother, Mary, other women, and even a beloved disciple are right near the cross (John 19:25–27).

Contradiction:

This wouldn't be allowed by Roman soldiers, especially not for rebels.

Also odd that none of the male disciples are arrested for association.

Final Words from the Cross

Historical Fact:

Crucifixion victims could barely breathe, let alone speak in the view of some interpreters.

Most would groan, gurgle, or scream — no long speeches.

Bible Claim:

Jesus gives seven distinct statements (depending on the Gospel), including:

"It is finished."

(John 19:30)

"Father, forgive them..." (Luke 23:34)

"My God, why have you forsaken me?"

(Mark 15:34)

Contradiction:

A man dying of crucifixion cannot articulate speeches, especially if he was scourged beforehand.

Earthquake, Darkness, and Veil Tearing

Historical Fact:

No Roman or Jewish historian (like Philo, Josephus, or Tacitus) mentions a massive earthquake, sky turning dark, or temple veil tearing during Jesus' crucifixion.

Bible Claim:

All these happen the moment Jesus dies (Matthew 27:45–51).

Contradiction:

These apocalyptic signs seem literary, symbolic, and unconfirmed by external sources.

Different Crucifixion Accounts

The four Gospels all tell different stories about Jesus' crucifixion:

Mark: "Why have you forsaken me?" - Simon of Cyrene carries cross - Silent mostly

Luke:

"Father forgive them..." and "Into your hands..." - Simon carries cross - Talkative

Matthew: Same as Mark - Simon carries cross - Silent

John: "It is finished." - Jesus carries it himself - Talks to mother, disciple

Contradiction:

Either Jesus carried his cross or he didn't.

Either he was silent and abandoned, or calm and forgiving — can't be all of them.

The Two Thieves

Bible Conflict:

In Matthew 27:44 and Mark 15:32, both thieves mock Jesus.

But in Luke 23:39–43, one mocks, the other defends Jesus and is promised paradise.

Contradiction:

Contradictory depiction of a key moment, suggesting literary invention.

Conclusion

There is no reason to believe the ' crucifixion, as told in the Gospels, follows Roman historical practice. It:

Contradicts Roman legal procedures

Violates burial customs

Depicts unrealistic behavior of soldiers and victims

Is filled with symbolic and contradictory elements across Gospels

It appears more like a crafted theological narrative designed to fulfill prophecy and portray Jesus as a divine martyr — not a real event recorded with accuracy.

Earliest Non-Biblical Accounts of Jesus

Josephus

(c. 93–94 CE) — Jewish Historian

In Antiquities of the Jews, Book 18, Josephus mentions a man named Jesus:

"About this time lived Jesus, a wise man... a worker of amazing deeds... condemned to the cross by Pilate... His followers did not cease... the tribe of Christians, named after him, has not disappeared."

Most scholars betray the core of this Testimonium Flavianum is authentic, though later Christian scribes may have embellished it.

1. If a text is embellished, is it still trustworthy?

Not really — and here's why:

If Christian scribes added details later, we can't separate the core from the contamination.

If no contemporary (30s–40s CE) source mentions Jesus, and the first mention is 60+ years later (Tacitus), then there's zero contemporaneous evidence.

Every single text is secondhand or hostile hearsay, meaning no one alive during Jesus's supposed ministry wrote anything about him.

That's not what we'd expect for a man supposedly:

Performing mass healings and public miracles

Drawing huge crowds

Causing civic unrest

Raising the dead

Coming back from the grave

Nothing in Roman or Jewish records from the 30s–40s CE says a word about any of that.

2. Were crucifixions common? Yes.

Crucifixion was a very real, severe Roman execution method, used extensively:

71 BCE: Spartacus' revolt – 6,000 crucified along Appian Way

4 BCE: Judas of Galilee's rebellion – 2,000 crucified

1st century CE: Josephus records mass crucifixions during Jewish War

70 CE: Siege of Jerusalem – thousands crucified by Titus Source: Josephus, Jewish War

(written c. 75–79 CE)

"So many were crucified that there was no room for the crosses, and no crosses for the bodies." – Jewish War 5.11.1

So yes, crucifixion was common for:

Political rebels

Slaves and lower-class criminals

Messianic claimants (yes, plural)

3. Other Stories of Crucified or Dying-Rising Figures

Many ancient religions and mythologies had dying-rising gods or divine men killed unjustly, and some involved executions:

Inanna (Sumerian): Descends into underworld, killed, hung on a hook, resurrected (~2000 BCE)

Osiris (Egyptian): Dismembered and resurrected by Isis (Ancient, pre-Jesus)

Prometheus (Greek): Bound to a rock, tortured daily by Zeus (~5th c. BCE)

Zalmoxis (Thracian): God of immortality, disappears for 3 years, returns (Herodotus ~5th c. BCE)

Romulus (Roman myth): Mysteriously vanishes, later "appears" in divine form (Pre-Jesus)

Dionysus: Killed and reborn, often linked with suffering and resurrection (Pre-Jesus)

Importantly: Crucifixion itself wasn't a common element in myth, but violent death and divine resurrection absolutely were.

4. So what could have happened?

Here's one plausible reconstruction grounded in severe realism:

There were multiple failed messiahs in 1st-century Judea (e. g., Theudas, the Egyptian prophet, Judas of Galilee).

Some of them were crucified by the Romans — a political warning.

A small cult may have formed around one such figure — possibly named Yeshua — after his death.

His story, over 40–70 years, became mythologized:

Miracles added

Birth narrative invented (Matthew and Luke contradict)

Resurrection story modeled on mythic templates (Osiris, Horus,

Dionysus)

Jesus eventually deified (as seen in Paul's letters)

By the time of Josephus (~93 CE) and Tacitus (~116 CE), the myth had hardened into "fact", but no one alive at the time of Jesus ever wrote about him.

Conclusion

Crucifixion was real and widely used by the Romans — but not unique to Jesus.

The earliest references to Jesus are late secondhand accounts, and tainted by agenda or later editing.

Multiple ancient myths involved dying and rising gods, many with more evidence than Jesus.

It is very possible that "Jesus" as we know him is a theological construction, built on scraps of a forgotten rebel, layered with myth and propaganda over decades.

JESUS WHO?

Jesus Who? Historical Evidence for Jesus

Tacitus - Annals, written c. 116 CE

Roman historian Tacitus references Christus being executed under Pontius Pilate. Born c. 56 CE, Tacitus wrote nearly a century after the alleged crucifixion and never met Jesus. The term "procurator" is anachronistic — Pilate was a "prefect." No evidence confirms Tacitus used Roman records; the passage likely reflects Christian hearsay circulating decades after Jesus would have died.

Josephus - Antiquities of the Jews, c. 93–94 CE

Jewish historian Josephus allegedly described Jesus in the Testimonium Flavianum. Born 37 CE, Josephus never met Jesus and wrote over 60 years after the alleged events. The passage contains Christian phrases like "He was the Christ," widely viewed as later interpolations. Scholarly consensus holds the text was altered by Christian scribes.

Pliny the Younger - Letter to Emperor Trajan, c. 112 CE

Pliny writes about Christians worshipping Christ "as a god."

This is not a historical account of Jesus, but a reference to

Christian rituals. Pliny does not claim Jesus existed, nor does he name sources. His knowledge is derived from interrogations of Christians, writing over 80 years after Jesus would have died.

Suetonius - Life of Claudius, c. 121 CE

Suetonius states that Claudius expelled Jews from Rome due to disturbances caused by "Chrestus." "Chrestus" may be a misspelling of "Christus" or an entirely different person. There is no mention of Jesus, Christianity, crucifixion, or Galilee. The name was common, and the timeline is too early.

Lucian of Samosata - The Death of Peregrinus, c. 170 CE

Lucian mocks Christians for worshipping a "crucified sage." Lucian was a satirist writing over a century after Jesus' alleged death. His account is not based on historical investigation but on Christian beliefs. He never cites sources or details that confirm historicity.

Mara bar Serapion - Letter to his son, 1st to 3rd century CE

Refers to a "wise king" of the Jews who was executed. The figure is never named. No mention of Jesus, crucifixion, or resurrection. The letter is philosophical, not historical, and can refer to multiple possible figures. The date is uncertain and likely post-Christian.

The pattern is clear: every single reference to Jesus comes from writers who lived decades or centuries after his supposed death. None of these authors could have been eyewitnesses or had direct knowledge of Jesus. Their accounts reflect Christian beliefs and stories that had circulated for generations, not contemporary historical records.

THE MYTH BEFORE THE MYTH:

The Myth Before the Myth: Ancestral Shadows of the Christ Figure

Long before the gospels, the ancient world echoed with stories of gods who died, descended, and returned again — deities who offered salvation, taught divine mysteries, healed the sick, and triumphed over death. Though none of these myths mirror the Jesus story point for point, they form a cultural and mythological foundation from which early Christian motifs likely grew. Here, we examine only those ancient precedents that scholars and historians accept as authentic, attested, and relevant.

  1. Dionysus (Greece)

Born of a mortal woman (Semele) and Zeus, Dionysus was a divine son who walked among men. His mystery cults offered salvation and eternal life, with rituals involving wine (his blood) and sacred feasting. Called the "twice-born," Dionysus was torn apart (in some versions by the Titans), then restored to life. His followers reenacted his death and rebirth, being this united them with the god and promised immortality.

Sources: Euripides' Bacchae (5th c. BCE), Orphic hymns, Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch.

  1. Asclepius (Greece)

The divine healer son of Apollo, Asclepius was so skilled he could raise the dead, prompting Zeus to strike him down with a thunderbolt. Later deified and widely worshipped in temples as a savior, especially for the sick. Shrines to him were places of miraculous healing, involving prayers, dreams, and rituals.

Sources: Pausanias, Pindar, Plato's Republic, inscriptions from healing temples (Asclepieia).

  1. Romulus (Rome)

Born of a virgin priestess and the god Mars, Romulus was one of Rome's legendary founders. After a violent death (some say dismembered by the Senate), his body vanished, and he was said to have ascended to heaven. He reappeared in glorified form to a companion and proclaimed his divine identity.

Sources: Livy (Ab Urbe Condita), Plutarch (Romulus), Cicero.

  1. Zalmoxis (Thrace / Scythia)

A mysterious figure among the Getae, Zalmoxis taught that death is not the end. According to Herodotus, he disappeared into a cave for three years, was thought dead, then reappeared alive, convincing followers of the afterlife. His legend involves teaching resurrection and immortality.

Sources: Herodotus (Histories 4.93–96), Plato (Charmides).

  1. Osiris (Egypt)

Killed and dismembered by his brother Set, Osiris was restored by Isis and became Lord of the Underworld. His story centers on death, resurrection, and divine kingship over the dead. Pharaohs were seen as Osiris incarnate, guaranteeing eternal life through ritual union with him.

Sources: Pyramid Texts (c. 2400 BCE), Plutarch's Isis and Osiris, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead.

  1. Inanna / Ishtar (Mesopotamia)

The goddess descended into the underworld, was stripped, judged, and killed, then resurrected and ascended. Her return marked the renewal of life, associated with the agricultural cycle.

Sources: The Descent of Inanna (Sumerian, 3rd millennium BCE), Ishtar's Descent (Akkadian).

No single myth is a carbon copy of the Jesus narrative — but when examined together, they reveal a shared archetypal pattern: the dying-and-returning god, the savior healer, the miracle-working divine man, the ascended son of god, the teacher of mysteries and eternal life. These stories circulated in the Eastern Mediterranean for centuries before Christianity arose. They formed the cultural atmosphere into which the Jesus story was born, shaped, and elevated.

This makes them not identical to Jesus, but ancestral in mythic form — the blueprint before the figure. Every element included here is textually attested, archaeologically grounded, and endorsed by mainstream scholarship. This is not coincidence — it is literary evolution.

Conclusion

This examination has surveyed various textual, historical, and interpretive challenges that biblical scholars have identified within the Hebrew Bible and New Testament. These issues—including apparent contradictions, editorial layers, mythological parallels, and historical discrepancies—represent genuine areas of ongoing scholarly investigation and debate. The evidence presented here reflects the consensus findings of critical biblical scholarship regarding textual difficulties, compositional history, and historical context. However, it is important to note that these observations admit multiple interpretations and have generated diverse scholarly responses. Conservative scholars have developed sophisticated explanations for many of these challenges, including harmonization strategies, contextual interpretations, and alternative translation approaches. Many believing scholars argue that textual difficulties do not necessarily undermine claims of divine inspiration, noting that inspiration can work through human authors and historical processes. Critical scholars tend to view these patterns as evidence of natural literary development, reflecting the human origins and cultural contexts of biblical texts. They often interpret apparent contradictions and editorial layers as indicators of multiple sources and lengthy compositional histories. The relationship between textual criticism and theological interpretation remains complex. Some scholars maintain that rigorous historical-critical analysis is compatible with religious faith, while others see such analysis as challenging traditional claims about biblical authority and inspiration. Readers must evaluate this evidence within their own interpretive frameworks, considering both the scholarly observations presented here and the broader questions of hermeneutics, religious epistemology, and the relationship between historical investigation and theological conviction. The questions raised by critical biblical scholarship—regardless of one's conclusions—demonstrate the importance of serious engagement with the texts' historical contexts, literary development, and interpretive challenges. Such engagement enriches our understanding of these influential documents, whether approached from academic, religious, or cultural perspectives

Glossary

Adonai - Hebrew word meaning "Lord" or "Master," used as a substitute for YHWH when Jews considered the divine name too sacred to pronounce.

Almah - Hebrew word meaning "young woman" or "maiden," often mistranslated as "virgin" in Isaiah 7:14 prophecies.

Apocrypha - Collection of biblical books included in some Christian traditions but excluded from others, particularly Protestant canons.

Dead Sea Scrolls - Ancient Jewish manuscripts discovered near Qumran (1947-1956) dating from approximately 200 BCE to 70 CE, providing insight into Second Temple Judaism. Deuteronomy - Fifth book of the Torah, whose composition and dating remain subjects of scholarly debate, with some portions potentially written during or after the Babylonian exile.

Disfellowshipping - Jehovah's Witness practice of formally expelling members and requiring other members to shun them completely.

Documentary Hypothesis - Scholarly theory that the Torah was compiled from multiple sources (J, E, D, P) rather than written by a single author.

El Elyon - Hebrew name meaning "God Most High," used in some biblical passages as distinct from YHWH, suggesting ancient polytheistic origins.

Enuma Elish - Babylonian creation epic (c. 1750-1100 BCE) that shares structural similarities with Genesis creation accounts.

Epigraph - Quotation placed at the beginning of a book or chapter to suggest its theme.

Firmament - English translation of Hebrew "raqia," referring to the solid dome of ancient cosmology placed over the flat earth.

Gilgamesh, Epic of - Ancient Mesopotamian epic containing a flood narrative remarkably similar to the biblical account of Noah.

Ha-satan - Hebrew term meaning "the adversary" or "the accuser," referring to a role or function rather than a proper name for a fallen angel.

Holokautoma - Greek word in the Septuagint meaning "whole burnt offering," indicating complete incineration, used in Jephthah's vow.

Jehovah - Anglicized form of YHWH created by medieval Christian scholars who mistakenly combined the consonants of YHWH with the vowels of Adonai.

LXX - Roman numeral abbreviation for Septuagint, referring to the traditional seventy translators.

Masoretic Text - Traditional Hebrew text of the Bible, standardized by Jewish scribes (Masoretes) between the 7th-10th centuries CE.

Messiah - Hebrew "mashiach," meaning "anointed one," referring to a divinely appointed leader, later appointed specifically to Jesus in Christian theology.

Nazirite - Individuals bound by specific vows including abstaining from wine and avoiding contact with corpses, as described in Numbers 6.

Paleography - Study of ancient writing systems and manuscripts to determine their age, origin, and authenticity.

Pseudepigrapha - Writings falsely attributed to biblical figures, common in ancient Jewish and Christian literature.

Qumran - Archaeological site near the Dead Sea where the Dead Sea Scrolls were discovered.

Footnotes are universally preferred over endnotes

Every major biblical studies journal examined uses footnotes exclusively, rejecting the endnote format common in some other academic fields. The Journal of Biblical Literature, Biblical Interpretation, New Testament Studies, and other top-tier publications consistently employ footnote formatting [JSTOR +2](https://www. jstor. org/journal/jbibllite) for immediate reference accessibility. High footnoting density reflects scholarly expectations

Biblical criticism requires exceptionally thorough documentation compared to many academic fields. Articles typically range from 5,000-10,000 words with 20-100+ footnotes, [Sage Journals](https://journals. sagepub.

com/author-instructions/jot) creating citation densities that exceed most humanities disciplines.

This intensive footnoting serves multiple scholarly functions: demonstrating engagement with multilingual secondary literature, providing textual evidence for interpretive claims, citing manuscript variants that affect translation decisions, and acknowledging alternative scholarly positions. The field's historical development across German, French, and English scholarship traditions means comprehensive research requires accessing sources in multiple languages. Different sub-fields within biblical studies show varying citation patterns: exegetical articles analyzing specific passages require heavy biblical text citation and manuscript evidence; historical-critical studies need extensive archaeological and ancient source documentation; theological interpretation articles integrate both biblical texts and interpretive traditions, often producing the highest footnote densities.
