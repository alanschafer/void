<section id="dedication" class="dedication">

<p class="c"><i>For everyone who lost their last goodbye.</i></p>
</section>
<section id="opening">

<p>A double-fatal crash happened at about 7:15 p.m. on Monday, July 9, 2018, on Division Street at Second Avenue South in Waite Park. A Chevrolet Malibu was heading west at a high rate of speed when it went through the median, struck a sign, crossed into the eastbound lanes the wrong way, and hit a 1970 Chevrolet El Camino head-on.</p>
<p><b>The men in the El Camino.</b> Roger Nelson, 77, of Clearwater, owned the El Camino. It was a car that had won trophies, and the people who knew him said he loved it. His family described him as a retired truck driver, a father of four, and a grandfather. Richard Tomlinson, 61, of Grantsburg, Wisconsin, was in town to test-drive it because he was thinking of buying it. Tomlinson was driving, and he died at the scene. Nelson was the passenger. He was taken to St. Cloud Hospital, where he died.</p>
<p><b>The Malibu’s driver.</b> Charlie Barnett, 28, of St. Cloud, was driving on a revoked license. Officers found an aerosol bottle of dusting cleaner on the floor on the driver’s side of the Malibu, and witnesses said he did not slow down or try to avoid the crash. After the crash he was taken to St. Cloud Hospital with injuries that were not considered life-threatening.</p>
<p><b>The outcome.</b> Barnett was charged with two counts of criminal vehicular homicide. He later pleaded guilty to both counts. It was a Norgaard plea, which is when a defendant accepts that the evidence shows guilt even though he has no memory of the incident himself. He was scheduled to be sentenced on January 17.</p>
<p>I was there that day, and this is how it went.</p>
</section>
<section id="prelude">
<h2>Prelude</h2>
<p>I started that day the way I started most days: I went to work. I was new at the job, a pipeline fuser. That was an unusual jump. Usually you start as a laborer and work your way up to fuser, but I went straight to fuser. I was learning on the job, because I had failed the test. I was two months in after training.</p>
<p>Before the pipeline, I worked at a grocery store, off and on, for about ten years. The pipeline was the best job I had ever been part of. It paid $25 an hour, at a time when you couldn’t get $9 an hour at a grocery store, so I went for it. That is what brought me to St. Cloud.</p>
<p>A fuser is the plumber of the job. On an average day, you wake up earlier than the crew and get to the shop to prepare the materials you need. If you don’t do that, you’re really fucking up.</p>
<p>Then you go around the job site, connecting the pipes that people have dug up, whether it’s the main line, the services, or the houses.</p>
<p>The crew was horrible. They acted like my friend in private, and in a group setting I was the butt of every joke.</p>
<p>I was proud that I got to be a fuser. It was a really lofty goal, and I was surprised I was able to do it. It turns out you can do all sorts of things when you stop limiting yourself.</p>
<p>For a few weeks I had been trying to work out how to live sustainably on the pipeline while on the road, so I bought a camper instead of staying in motels.</p>
<p>After work that day I went to Fleet Farm in Waite Park, looking for a generator to power it.</p>
<p>I left disappointed, and angry. I got in the truck and put it in drive. Then I got a text message, so I put it in park, answered it quickly, and put it in drive again. It was from my girlfriend, who is my wife now.</p>
<p>It took me a minute to respond, but after I did, I took off. If I hadn’t answered that message, it could have been me.</p>
</section>
<section id="chapter-1-the-events">
<h2>Chapter 1: The Events</h2>
<p>After I answered the text, I headed toward St. Cloud with Best Buy on my right. I was three cars back from the El Camino.</p>
<p>It was a nice evening. I had the AC on in my truck and the stereo going, so I didn’t hear a thing.</p>
<p>The first thing I remember is the Malibu in the air, out of the corner of my eye. The statement I gave the next day says I saw it swerve a few seconds before the impact. I don’t remember that. I remember thinking, there’s no way that’s real. Then I heard people running past me, screaming, and I looked up.</p>
<p>From where it ended up, I’d say the Malibu was going at least sixty.</p>
<p>Every lane was stopped. I had planned to drive around it, but there was no way around. I got out.</p>
<p>I walked up casually. There was a circle of people, a lot of them not getting involved, and I pushed my way through.</p>
<p>The people around me were in disbelief, losing their minds. The ones who surrounded the scene were really quiet.</p>
<p>Everyone was gathered around the Malibu, trying to get the driver out. I thought, they’ve got it covered. So I went to the El Camino.</p>
<p>It was a classic, red with black stripes, in mint condition until a moment before. Now the whole thing was mangled. I didn’t know either man. The driver had become part of the engine. I learned later that he was Richard Tomlinson, who was 61.</p>
<p>There was a river of blood flowing onto the ground after I was done kneeling down beside the driver. I followed the river of blood methodically, and when I reached the head, fifteen feet or so ahead of the vehicle, I turned around and looked at the scene: the people going crazy, the collision, the chaos. And I was ecstatic. I stood there and took it in. I appreciated it. I thought it was the greatest thing I had ever experienced. Everyone around me was in terror, and I was in glee and bliss, and I couldn’t understand the discrepancy.</p>
<p>I should have taken pictures. I’ve always wanted to be a photographer, I think, but it never crossed my mind. So I tried to burn the sight into my brain, and I did.</p>
<p>Then I went around to the passenger side. A man was there, head bowed. He wasn’t breathing. His hands stayed where they were. I sat with him for a minute or three. I told him, “I’m really sorry you’re going through this. I hear that death is the greatest trip.”</p>
<p>He was pronounced dead at the hospital. His name was Roger Nelson, and he was 77. The El Camino was his. I believe he was gone long before the hospital.</p>
<p>I realized goodbyes were hard when I understood that he had said goodbye to his family for the last time, and he didn’t know it.</p>
<p>Then I went back up to the Malibu. The flames had started, and people were working the driver’s door. I pushed some of them aside and said, “Let me give it a try.” I pulled that door three times, and it wouldn’t open. It was all crinkled.</p>
<p>I realized the driver’s door was never going to open. The only other option was the passenger side.</p>
<p>For some reason, no one was over there. That side was in mint condition, and the door opened like normal.</p>
<p>I thought it was going to explode. That’s why I did it. It smelled like burning plastic, and it was a very small fire, so I didn’t feel any heat.</p>
<p>I thought for a moment about whether I should get in there. Right as I was about to get into the car, I said out loud, fuck it, and jumped in the car.</p>
<p>I went in through the passenger door. The driver was very discombobulated, very confused, and he was trying to get out through his own door. I said, “You’ve got to come with me.” He kept resisting. He kept saying he was waiting for his cousin to come get him out. I said, “No. I’m going to get you out of here.”</p>
<p>I grabbed him around the chest, below the armpits, because I didn’t want to break anything. I undid his seatbelt and pulled him out. He screamed, and I thought that was kind of funny.</p>
<p>I walked him to the paramedics. He was in shock. When I set him down, he started wailing, and he looked like he was in agony. I watched him writhing in pain and wailing and felt slight amusement at how he oscillated his tone, so I stepped back and let the paramedics treat him. There were aerosol cans on the floor of the car. I learned later that he had been intoxicated.</p>
<p>I stepped onto the sidewalk and watched the scene, having my own high. I was running on adrenaline, but it never registered as adrenaline. It just felt like a really chill time.</p>
<p>When I stepped onto the sidewalk, an older guy was standing there, and he initiated a conversation.</p>
<p>“I saw what you did,” he said. “That was really something.”</p>
<p>“Oh, it was nothing,” I said. “It was just a thing.”</p>
<p>“This is going to stick with you,” he said.</p>
<p>“No, no, it won’t.”</p>
<p>“Yes, it will.”</p>
<p>I thought, no. But it did.</p>
<p>I stayed on the sidewalk until I could leave, which didn’t take long.</p>
<p class="note"><em>A note on the record. I gave a statement to the Waite Park police the day after. It and my memory agree on most things. It has me going to the Malibu first. In my memory, I went to the El Camino first, and that is the order I have told it in.</em></p>
</section>
<section id="chapter-2-the-aftermath">
<h2>Chapter 2: The Aftermath</h2>
<p>I never knew how right the pedestrian was.</p>
<h3 id="two-days-after">Two days after</h3>
<p>I had finally gotten a generator. It was so shitty. It was louder inside the camper than outside, it moved around, and it only lasted about an hour. I packed up that night and suffered through it. The next night I couldn’t handle it. I went driving.</p>
<p>I was on a long road with many curves, to nowhere in particular. Everything was super fucked and I had nothing to do. I couldn’t sleep, because of the insomnia and because of the generator. I got to the point where I felt like my head was going to explode, and I was weeping bitterly, and I started screaming as I drove.</p>
<p>I then experienced an out-of-body experience from an aerial view of my own truck, following myself from above while driving.</p>
<p>After that, everything was different.</p>
<h3 id="the-first-year">The first year</h3>
<p>The first year on the pipeline was really good, up until the incident. Then I could no longer sleep. I had insomnia constantly. I would oversleep for work.</p>
<p>I got worse progressively, but slowly. The next year I got on the pipeline again, and that year I got unemployment. In 2020, my wife and I had our first child. Then I just worked for years.</p>
<h3 id="the-quiet-years">The quiet years</h3>
<p>I worked at a commercial tire shop for three years.</p>
<p>I wasn’t thinking about the accident. I just moved on. But slowly, through those years, it drove itself back to me.</p>
<h3 id="the-tape">The tape</h3>
<p>Something drove me to get the tape of my statement. I couldn’t ignore it anymore. I went to St. Cloud and bought it for twenty dollars.</p>
<p>On the drive down, I was excited to hear my own words describe the incident.</p>
<p>I listened to the tape twice.</p>
<p>After I listened to the tape, I felt a shift in my mind.</p>
<p>When I heard it, I simulated in my mind what that night would have been like in a different light, with the fun filter removed and only the horror remaining. It was more than my mind could handle. I felt my mind break, and then my memory problems really started.</p>
<h3 id="leftover-panic">Leftover panic</h3>
<p>I have had dissociative episodes my whole life, since I was a child.</p>
<p>The accident unlocked something. When a trauma is similar to an earlier one, it can open the earlier one up. This one opened a memory from before I could form memories, when I was two or three.</p>
<p>Heavy breathing. Someone about to attack me. Then being chased into a closet at home, and being completely cornered.</p>
<h3 id="the-fall">The fall</h3>
<p>Around the fall of 2023, the episodes got severely worse. By January 2024, the memories were bringing me to the floor. My head would spin, I’d sweat, and I’d get flashes of memory I couldn’t recognize.</p>
</section>
<section id="chapter-3-unscheduled">
<h2>Chapter 3: Unscheduled</h2>
<p>This chapter is what I do now. It is my experience, not medical advice.</p>
<h3 id="a-hard-day">A hard day</h3>
<p>On a day when it’s hard to tell what’s real, I stay in the present moment. I go with the flow and don’t do anything too extreme.</p>
<p>I meditate daily.</p>
<h3 id="there-is-no-script">There is no script</h3>
<p>We go through life thinking we know the future. We think our day is going to go a certain way. But it can go totally different and totally off script. That’s the point. There is no script. We think we perceive this internal script, and when things go off the rails, we lose our minds. But there was never a script that you were following.</p>
<p>Each and every moment going forward is unscheduled. I’m in a place I’ve never been, and that isn’t inherently bad. It’s just different. It’s a place, and it can’t be judged as the wrong place or a bad one.</p>
<p>If you have been through something like this, I would just say this: don’t pay too much attention to a schedule, because things can go against it very quickly.</p>
<h3 id="why-i-dont-say-goodbye">Why I don’t say goodbye</h3>
<p>I don’t like saying goodbye. Any goodbye could be the last one.</p>
<p>The man in the passenger seat said goodbye to his family for the last time, and he didn’t know it.</p>
<p>I’d rather leave it with an okay to say goodbye.</p>
</section>
